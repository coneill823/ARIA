# What a project — and a business — needs considered

The research behind A.R.I.A.'s business plans. When she develops an idea
she writes three things besides the plan: a **business plan**, a list of
**everything a complete proposal needs** (and what is still missing), and
**who does what** — the work she can do, and the work that is yours. This
page is where the questions in those documents come from, with the sources.

It is a checklist of things to consider, not advice on any one of them.
The rules on tax, registration, licences and data differ from country to
country — set where you are based (Settings → Pipeline → *Where you are
based*) and treat what she writes on those points as a list of things to
check, not as the answer.

## The questions

### 1. Is it worth doing at all?

- **Whose problem is it, and how do you know they have it?** The most
  common reason given for start-ups failing is building something nobody
  needed: CB Insights' analysis of founders' post-mortems put "no market
  need" first, and its more recent review of failed venture-backed
  companies names poor product-market fit as the main cause *behind*
  running out of money
  ([CB Insights](https://cbinsights.com/research/startup-failure-reasons-top),
  [Fortune's summary](https://fortune.com/2014/09/25/why-startups-fail-according-to-their-founders/)).
- **Evidence before building.** Steve Blank's customer development puts
  *customer discovery* and *customer validation* before any scaling: the
  facts are outside the building, and the steps are repeated until the
  model holds
  ([Customer development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_development)).
- **Ask in a way that gets true answers.** *The Mom Test* (Rob
  Fitzpatrick): talk about their life rather than your idea, ask about
  specifics in the past rather than hypotheticals about the future, talk
  less and listen more. "I would buy that" is not evidence; "the last
  time this happened I paid for…" is
  ([summary](https://www.shortform.com/blog/what-is-the-mom-test/),
  [notes](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/)).

*In her documents:* the business plan's first section says whose problem
it is and what the evidence is — and where there is none, "talk to five
people who have this problem" goes on **your** list, with the questions
she can prepare for you.

### 2. Who else is there, and how big is it?

- **Competitors and alternatives** — including "they do nothing" and
  "they use a spreadsheet". Public guidance on business plans asks for
  who your customers are, the size of your market and how it will grow,
  and who your competitors are. GOV.UK's own page is a hub of templates
  and guides; the sections are spelled out in the guides around it
  ([GOV.UK: Write a business plan](https://www.gov.uk/write-business-plan),
  [mygov.scot](https://www.mygov.scot/write-business-plan)).
- **Market size, in three layers.** *TAM* is all the demand there is;
  *SAM* the part your product, place and channels can reach; *SOM* the
  part you can realistically win. Size it top-down (start broad, narrow)
  and bottom-up (customers × price) and treat the gap between the two as
  the range. Writers define SOM slightly differently, so say which
  definition you use
  ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/tam-sam-som),
  [TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/enterprise-software/definition/TAM-SAM-SOM)).

### 3. What exactly is it, and how does it earn?

- **The model on one page.** The Business Model Canvas describes how a
  business creates, delivers and captures value in nine blocks: customer
  segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue
  streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost
  structure ([Strategyzer](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/what-is-a-business-model),
  [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_Model_Canvas)).
- **The same page for something that does not exist yet.** Ash Maurya's
  Lean Canvas keeps customer segments, unique value proposition,
  channels, revenue streams and cost structure, and swaps the rest for
  *problem*, *solution*, *key metrics* and *unfair advantage* — its
  purpose is to break an idea into the assumptions that need testing. A
  test for "unfair advantage": if a competitor could copy it with money
  or time, it is not one
  ([Lean Canvas](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-canvas/),
  [Atlassian's guide](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence/resources/guides/how-to/lean-canvas)).

### 4. Does the money work?

- **Break-even.** Units to break even = fixed costs ÷ (price per unit −
  variable cost per unit). It assumes price and costs hold still, so it
  is a first check, not a forecast
  ([Chase](https://www.chase.com/business/knowledge-center/grow/how-to-calculate-break-even-point),
  [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/books/principles-managerial-accounting/pages/3-2-calculate-a-break-even-point-in-units-and-dollars)).
- **Unit economics.** What a customer is worth over their lifetime (LTV)
  against what it costs to win one (CAC), and how many months it takes
  to earn the acquisition cost back. About 3:1 is the usual rule of
  thumb for LTV:CAC in software; the ratio alone can mislead — a good
  ratio with a two-year payback is worse for cash than a modest one
  with eight months
  ([Mercury](https://mercury.com/blog/understanding-unit-economics),
  [Visible](https://visible.vc/blog/unit-economics-for-startups/)).
- **Cash, month by month.** Lenders' templates ask for a cash-flow
  forecast — money in and out over the next twelve months — and, for a
  founder, a personal survival budget beside the business plan
  ([Start Up Loans](https://www.startuploans.co.uk/business-plan/)).
  Fuller plans add projections further out; guides differ on how far —
  three years is common, some ask for five
  ([guide to the SBA format](https://fitsmallbusiness.com/sba-business-plan-template/)).
- **Funding.** If money is being asked for: how much, from whom, for
  what, and how it comes back — check whether the funder has a template
  of its own before writing yours.

### 5. Is it allowed, and is it protected?

These are the points that differ most between countries. The examples
are from the United Kingdom.

- **Legal structure and registration.** Most businesses register as a
  sole trader or a limited company, and the choice changes tax and
  personal liability. A sole trader registers with the tax authority; a
  company is a separate legal entity registered with the company
  registrar. It is usually easier to go from sole trader to company than
  back ([GOV.UK: Set up a business](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business),
  [business structures](https://www.gov.uk/business-legal-structures)).
- **Licences, tax, insurance.** What applies depends on the trade and
  the place; some licences take weeks, so they go early on the list
  ([GOV.UK licences](https://www.gov.uk/browse/business/licences)).
- **Personal data.** If the project holds data about people, data
  protection law applies however small it is. The UK regulator's
  self-assessment for sole traders and small businesses walks through
  who is responsible, the lawful basis for holding the data, where it
  came from, who receives it, how long it is kept, how people can see
  and correct it — and whether a registration fee is due
  ([ICO checklist](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/checklists/assessment-for-small-business-owners-and-sole-traders/),
  [15 things to know](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/news-blogs-and-events/blogs/15-things-all-small-businesses-need-to-know-about-data-protection/)).
- **Intellectual property.** Copyright is automatic; patents have to be
  applied for; designs and trade marks exist unregistered and are
  stronger registered. Check the name is free before building a brand on
  it ([GOV.UK: Intellectual property and your work](https://www.gov.uk/intellectual-property-an-overview)).
- **Software licences.** Code published with no licence is not "free to
  use": default copyright applies and nobody may copy, distribute or
  build on it. Choosing a licence — and checking the licences of what
  the project itself depends on — is a decision, not a formality
  ([GitHub: licensing a repository](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/licensing-a-repository),
  [choosealicense.com](https://choosealicense.com/),
  [Open Source Guide](https://opensource.guide/legal/)).
- **Accessibility.** WCAG 2.2 is the reference for anything with a
  screen: content has to be perceivable, operable, understandable and
  robust, with testable criteria at levels A, AA and AAA
  ([W3C: WCAG 2 overview](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/)).

### 6. Can it be built, and run?

- **Feasibility on five counts** (TELOS): *technical* — can it be done
  with the technology and skills available; *economic* — can it be
  afforded and will it pay; *legal*; *operational* — does it fit how
  things are run; *scheduling* — can it be done in time
  ([Feasibility study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feasibility_study)).
- **Scope that can give.** MoSCoW sorts what is wanted into must have,
  should have, could have and won't have this time; the DSDM guidance is
  that must-haves take no more than about 60% of the effort, so there is
  something to drop when time runs short
  ([MoSCoW method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MoSCoW_method),
  [the 60% guidance, quoting the DSDM handbook](https://digitalcommunications.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/2016/08/05/moscow-prioritisation-is-on-effort/)).
- **Who does what.** A RACI chart gives every piece of work someone
  *responsible* (does it), exactly one *accountable* (owns the outcome),
  and names who is *consulted* and who is *informed*
  ([Responsibility assignment matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsibility_assignment_matrix)).
  A.R.I.A.'s version is deliberately simple: **hers** and **yours**.

### 7. What could go wrong?

- **A risk register**: each risk with how likely it is, what it would
  do, what is being done about it, and who owns it
  ([Risk register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_register)).
- **A premortem.** Before starting, assume the project has already
  failed and write down why. Gary Klein's point is that this finds the
  problems a "what might go wrong?" discussion is too polite to raise
  (Klein, *Performing a Project Premortem*, Harvard Business Review,
  September 2007;
  [his own account of the method](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/seeing-what-others-dont/202101/the-pre-mortem-method)).

### 8. When — and how will anyone know it worked?

- **Milestones that are outcomes**, each with a measure of success.
  This is how A.R.I.A.'s roadmap is already drawn.
- **Estimates from what actually happened.** People planning a piece of
  work look at the work itself and are too optimistic — the planning
  fallacy. The remedy is the outside view, *reference class
  forecasting*: estimate from how long similar work actually took
  ([PMI: Planning for the planning fallacy](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/planning-fallacy-causes-solutions-project-expectations-6374),
  [PMI: From Nobel Prize to project management](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068)).
  A.R.I.A.'s time estimates work this way: they come from the times
  measured on this machine, not from the planner's guess — see
  [AUTOPILOT.md](AUTOPILOT.md#how-long-will-it-take).

## The documents these questions end up in

| Document | What it is for | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| **Business plan — traditional** | lenders, investors, anyone who needs the detail | executive summary; company description; market analysis; organisation and management; service or product line; marketing and sales; funding request; financial projections; appendix ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/write-your-business-plan)) |
| **Business plan — lean** | starting quickly, a simple business, a plan that will change | typically one page; the nine parts usually given are the Business Model Canvas's — partnerships, activities, resources, value proposition, customer relationships, segments, channels, cost structure, revenue streams ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/write-your-business-plan), [the nine parts](https://www.masaischool.com/blog/how-to-write-a-business-plan-lean-canvas/)) |
| **Project charter** | authorising a project | objectives and success criteria, scope (in and out), deliverables, stakeholders, milestones, budget, risks, assumptions and constraints ([Project charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_charter)) |
| **Project proposal** | getting a yes | problem, proposed solution, objectives, approach, scope and deliverables, timeline, budget and resources, risks, success criteria ([QuillBot's outline](https://quillbot.com/blog/professional-writing/writing-a-project-proposal/), [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/project-management/how-to-write-project-proposal)) |
| **Business case** | justifying spending | the UK Treasury's five cases: strategic (is there a need), economic (is it value for money), commercial, financial and management ([GOV.UK business case guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/business-case-guidance-for-projects-and-programmes)) |
| **Grant application** | public innovation funding | typically: the need or challenge, approach and innovation, team and resources, market awareness, outcomes and route to market, wider impacts, project management, risks, added value ([Innovate UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/innovate-uk-open-funding-competition/open-programme-competition-guidance)) |

Advice that recurs across them: write the summary last; keep it short
enough to be read; and if it is for a funder, use the funder's template.

## How A.R.I.A. uses this

**The business plan** (`business-plan.md`, from
`system/templates/business-plan.md`) is the lean kind grown to the size
the idea deserves. Its sections follow the questions above: the problem
and who has it · the offer · market and alternatives · how it earns ·
reaching customers · what it takes to run · the money · rules and
protection · people · risks and what to test first · milestones and
measures · the verdict. Numbers are estimates with the arithmetic shown;
what she does not know is said, and finding it out becomes a task.

**What a complete proposal needs** is a fixed list — the union of what
the documents above ask for — and for each item she says whether it is
*there*, *there as an assumption that needs checking*, or *missing*.

**Who does what** splits the remaining work in two. *Hers*: what can be
researched, drafted, calculated or built — after you approve, these join
the project's requirements and autopilot does them. *Yours*: what takes
a person — talking to customers, choosing a name, registering, paying,
signing. For each of yours she notes what she can prepare so that your
part is short. She never ticks an item on your list.

## Limits

- This page was assembled from web searches in November 2026. The linked
  pages were found and summarised by search; not every one was read in
  full while writing this. Follow the link before relying on a detail.
- Nothing here, and nothing A.R.I.A. writes from it, is legal, tax or
  financial advice. A local model also gets facts wrong: figures in a
  business plan she drafts are starting points to check, and the plan
  says where they came from.
